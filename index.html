Fast Food Access

Fast food restaurants are highly concentrated around Australia's major cities and coastal regions, particularly along the east coast where population density is highest. Large clusters can be observed around Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, and Perth, while remote inland areas have far fewer outlets. Popular chains such as McDonald's, KFC, and Hungry Jack's are commonly located near shopping centres, highways, and tourist destinations to meet consumer demand.

Burgers and sandwiches account for the largest share of Australia's fast food market, followed by chicken and pizza-based outlets. These food categories dominate because they are widely available, convenient, and appeal to a broad range of consumers. Asian and Latin American fast food outlets also contribute significantly to the market, with popular chains such as Guzman y Gomez, Zambrero and Sushi Hub reflecting Australia's multicultural food preferences.

Fast Food and Obesity

Increasing fast food accessibility and consumption patterns may contribute to rising obesity rates and unhealthy lifestyle habits across Australia. Higher fast food consumption is often associated with diets high in calories, fat, and sugar, which may increase the risk of obesity and related health issues. Obesity statistics used in this dashboard were obtained from the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW).

Severe Obesity in Adults Over Time

Child and Adolescent Obesity Trends Over Time

Severe obesity among adults has increased steadily since 1995, with females consistently recording slightly higher rates than males. Across age groups, overweight and obesity are most common among middle-aged adults (45-64 years), while younger adults aged 18-24 show the lowest prevalence.

Although overweight prevalence declined between 2014-15 and 2017-18, AIHW reports that changes across this period were not statistically significant, suggesting the decrease may reflect normal survey variation rather than a substantial population-level improvement

Overweight and Obesity Rates Across Age Groups

Obesity Overweight

Young adults have a bigger difference between overweight and obesity rates, meaning many are above a healthy weight but not yet obese. As people get older, this gap gets smaller because obesity becomes more common. This pattern may be due to long term habits like unhealthy eating, frequent fast food consumption, and not getting enough exercise.

Overall, these three charts show that fast food access, eating habits, and physical activity are strongly linked to obesity in Australia. The rise in overweight and obesity rates may be due to frequent fast food consumption and not enough exercise, highlighting the need for healthier lifestyle choices.

Body Mass Index (BMI)

BMI is commonly used to estimate whether a person is underweight, healthy weight, overweight, or obese based on height and weight. Higher BMI levels may be linked to increased risks of obesity, diabetes, heart disease, and other long-term health conditions.

BMI and Obesity

Women have a higher proportion of individuals within the normal BMI range, while men are more likely to be overweight. Obesity is prevalent in both genders, indicating that excess weight remains a significant health concern among Australian adults.

The heat map reveals that most Australian adults are concentrated within the overweight and obesity BMI ranges rather than the underweight categories. This trend is observed for both males and females, suggesting that excess weight remains a widespread public health issue across the adult population.

Obesity and Physical Activity

Primary Health Networks (PHNs) are healthcare planning regions established to coordinate health services based on local population needs. Each PHN is identified by a specific PHN area code, and the boundaries are defined according to healthcare service regions rather than state borders. The map shows that physical inactivity levels differ across PHNs, suggesting that factors such as access to facilities, population characteristics, and lifestyle behaviours may influence activity levels.

The chart shows the distribution of weekly physical activity among males and females. Although many adults achieved 150 or more minutes of activity, a considerable proportion reported no activity. Lower activity levels are associated with increased risks of overweight and obesity, highlighting the importance of regular exercise for maintaining good health.

Conclusion

Overall, this dashboard shows that fast food access, consumption habits, obesity patterns, BMI distribution, and physical activity are closely connected to public health outcomes in Australia. Areas with higher fast food availability and lower physical activity may face greater health risks, highlighting the importance of balanced diets, active lifestyles, and stronger health awareness.
